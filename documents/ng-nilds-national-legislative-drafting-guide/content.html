Chapter 1:

Instructions and Consultations

  • 1.0 Understanding drafting instructions
  • 1.0.0 A drafting instruction is a request made by -
  • (a) a member of the National Assembly (NASS) or a State House of Assembly (SNA),
  • (b) Federal or State Governments,
  • (c) Ministries, Departments and Agencies (MDAs) of Federal or State Governments,
  • (d) private organisations,
  • (e) pressure groups and civil society organisations (CSOs), or
  • (f) any other person, head of department or office responsible for legislative drafting in the National and State Houses of Assembly, and Federal and State Ministries of Justice.
  • 1.1 Contents of the drafting instructions
  • 1.1.1 Drafting instructions should be in prose form and contain-
  • (a) sufficient background information;
  • (b) history of events which contributes to the solution proposed by the instructing officer;
  • (c) technical details, diagrams and tables;
  • (d) ful l outline of principal objects;

(h)

  • (e) a picture of how the legislation wi l l actually work in practice, the machinery envisaged and necessary powers and duties descrilaed in detail;
  • (f) complete, accurate, and comprehensive description in straightforward language, of how the objectives of the legislation wi l l be achieved;
  • (g) correspondences with agencies saddled with particular responsibi lity; problems currently facing the agency including gaps in the existing laws;
  • (i) means by which the objects are to be achieved;
  • (j) analysis of implications, challenges and difficulties of the proposal whether legal, cost benefits, social or administrative;
  • (k) specify and note alterations to existing laws, provisions of other Acts and regulations that will be consequentially affected;
  • (I) how contraventions of the legislation are intended to be dealt with;
  • (m) the nature of the proposal, timeframes and consultative process (recommendation, appointment and confirmation),
  • (n) reference to reading materials that would be helpful;
  • (o) reports of committees, law reform bodies or the like, on which the proposals are based;
  • (p) legislation in other jurisdictions that would provide useful guidance;
  • (q) discussion documents, texts, or articles;
  • (r) information about the availabi lity of aii relevant legal opinions and legal research;
  • (s) relevant court decisions or an Indication of their availability;
  • (t) any transitional provisions needed to deal with issues arising as a result of the repeal of one law and the enactment of new law;
  • (u) commencement date of all or part of the legislation and procedure, or information about how the law is to come into operation;
  • (v) Acts, Laws or Regulations to be amended and the nature of the amendment needed,
  • (w) any Act, Law or Regulation to be repealed; and
  • (x) the name and contact information for the senior official responsible for giving instructions and ansv/erlng questions.
  • 1.1.2 Where there are multiple Instructors, the drafter should identify the lead Instructor (portfolio department) who shal l liaise with other instructors.
  • 1.2 Role of the instructor
  • 1.2.1 The role of the Instructor Is to -
  • (a) take responsibility for policy matters;
  • (b) act as the primary point of contact for the drafter;
  • (c) provide clear drafting Instructions In plain prose language and give, as fully as possible, the background of the proposed bil l;
  • (d) state what existing legislation affect the subject matter of the proposed legislation;
  • (e) state Vv'hether the proposal is based on an existing legislation in Nigeria or in any other country and draw the drafter's attention to that legislation;
  • (f) where the proposed legislation seeks to establish a body, state the name, composition of its governing board and the functions, powers and special features of the body;
  • (g) specify the date the legislation is to come into operation, if it is different from the date of assent or passing of the bil l or, in the case of a subsidiary legislation, the date of signature.
  • 1.3 Analysing the instructions 1 .3.1 After receipt of the drafting instructions, the drafter should -
  • (a) study the instructions carefully to understand the purpose, objectives and policy behind the proposed legislation;
  • (b) ascertain the mischief, gap or defect intended to be cured by the proposed legislation; and
  • (c) consult the instructor for further details and clarification.
  • 1.4 The purpose of analysis
  • 1.4.1 In analysing the instruction, the drafter should consider the existing laws that directly or indirectly relate to the subject matter of the legislative proposal.
  • 1.4.2 The purpose of analysis is to -
  • (a) ensure that the instructions do not conflict with the provisions of the Constitution, Interpretation Act, existing legislation and international treaties or conventions to which Nigeria is a signatory;
  • (b) avoid duplication of legislation and overlap of functions between two or more agencies;
  • (c) ensure that the instructions do not infringe on the rules of natural justice and fundamental rights of the citizens;
  • (d) identify whether legislation is the appropriate instrument to address the issues at stake or alternative options like policy, public enlightenment, regulations, order, notice, guidelines; and
  • (e) comply with the drafting rules, style and convention.

Chapter 5; Plain English Approach
  • 5.0 Why Plain English?
  • 5.0.1 Plain language is clear intelligible English. It is simplistic English. It does not involve any loss of precision. In the legislative context, the principles^ plain language aim for clarity in the -
  • (a) language of legislation;
  • (b) structure of the legal ideas contained in legislation; and
  • (c) physical layout and presentation of legislation!
5.0.2 The use of plain English in legislation is intended to|

remove barriers to communication and make the law more accessible. It is important to recognise tha^ the proper use of plain English principles does not mean any loss of precision and does not result in the] introduction of any uncertainty.

  • 5.0.3 Legislation should be understood with a minimum effort by its users. To this end, a drafter should always| draft with the needs of intended users of legislation in mind. This is the first principle of effective plain English drafting.
  • 5.0.4 Other principles relate more specifically to -
  • (a) effective communication through logical and practical organisation of materials;
  • (b) simple sentence construction;
  • (c) careful choice of words; and
  • (d) practical physical design.
  • 5.0.5 These principles, and associated guidelines, include clarity, design, sentence structure and choice of words.
  • 5.1 Clarity
  • 5.1.1 Legislation should be organised in a way that assists and makes sense to the reader and, in particular -
  • (a) information should be divided into manageable and related pieces, and placed in a logical sequence, for example, by placing the -
  • (i) substantive before the procedural,
  • (ii) broadly applicable before the narrowly applicable,
  • (iii) general before the specific, and
  • (iv) rules before the exceptions;
  • (b) fundamental information should not be obscured by minor provisions;
  • (c) headings should be informative and help explain the structure, scope and effect of legislation; and
  • (d) unnecessary information (including unnecessary internal crossmeferencing) should be minimised or eliminated.
  • 5.2 Design
  • 5.2.1 Legislation should be physically designed so that it is clear and easy to understand and, in particular -
  • (a) typesetting and font sizes should be easy to read, blocks of capital letters (for example, in I
headings) should be restricted to the rules on|

capitalisation and be minimised;

  • (b) line length and section length should not be excessive;
  • (c) navigational aids, such as a table of contents, should be used;
  • (d) the numbering system should be clear and ea; to follow, for example -
  • (i) number each section in Arabic numerals, 2, 3... and end each section with a ful l stc (.),
  • (ii) number subsections in enclosed Arabic numerals, (1), (2), (3) and end each subsection with a ful l stop (.),
  • (iii) number paragraphs in enclosed alphabets, (a), (b),(c) and end each paragraph with a semi-colon (;) or comma),), depending on the sentence structure,
  • (iv) number subparagraphs in enclosed roman numerals, (i), (ii), (i i i) and end each sub paragraph with a comma),);
  • (e) subsections and paragraphs within sections should be systematically indented to highlight the hierarchies in the structure of information;
  • (f) examples, tables, diagrams and flow charts may be used as needed to help explain the text but they are restricted to the schedule; and

40

  • (g) marginal notes to sections should be as informative as possible.
  • 5.3 Sentence Structure
  • 5.3.1 Sentences should be structured in a way that allows information to be easily absorbed. Complex sentence structures should be avoided as far as possible and, in particular -
  • (a) sentences should be short in length and, generally, a sentence should not exceed 5 lines in length and should express one central idea;
  • (b) the division of a sentence into unnecessary paragraphs and subparagraphs ("shredding") should be avoided;
  • (c) modifying elements should not be complex and should be placed as near as possible to the sentence element they modify, and kept to a mini m u m;
  • (d) intrusive phrases and clauses, especially between the subject, verb, and object, should be avoided;
  • (e) the present tense should be preferred, as should the active to the passive voice, verbs to noun phrases and positive to negative expression. Layered negatives should be avoided; and
  • (f) ambiguity should be eliminated.
  • 5.4 Choice of words
  • 5.4.1 Language in legislation should be as clear and direct as possible while remaining precise and technically correct and, in particular -
