Part 1: Introduction

1.1: Role of Office of the Chief Parliamentary Counsel

1. The Office of the Chief Parliamentary Counsel is an Administrative Office in relation to the Department of Premier and Cabinet. The Office provides drafting services for the State of Victoria for legislation and also carries out the function of Government Printer in printing and publishing Victorian legislation.

2. In relation to statutory rules, OCPC has a specific statutory function under section 13 of the Subordinate Legislation Act 1994 for all statutory rules made by, or consented to or approved by, the Governor in Council. These are generally called "regulations". The Chief Parliamentary Counsel certifies that a proposed statutory rule is within power under its authorising Act and satisfies the other criteria listed in that section. The SLA requires that this certificate is issued before a statutory rule is put to the Governor in Council for making.

3. The matters considered by Chief Parliamentary Counsel go to the legality and effectiveness of the statutory rule and are considered in the course of settling any statutory rule. As part of this function OCPC settles, and in some cases, drafts, statutory rules.

4. These Notes for Guidance are designed to provide assistance as to OCPC's requirements regarding draft statutory rules prepared by you as Departmental officers for settling by OCPC. These Notes do not deal with legislative instruments within the meaning of the SLA.

5. If you have any general queries about the preparation of statutory rules, you can contact the Chief Parliamentary Counsel at OCPC on 9651 2103.

1.2: Definition of statutory rule in the Subordinate Legislation Act 1994

6. Statutory rule is defined in section 3(1) of the Subordinate Legislation Act 1994 as follows:

statutory rule means—

  • (a) a regulation—
  • (i) made by the Governor in Council; or
  • (ii) made with the consent or approval of the Governor in Council; or
  • (iii) which the Governor in Council has power to disallow— other than a regulation made by a local authority or by a person or body with jurisdiction limited to a district or locality or a local law made under Division 3 of Part 3 of the Local Government Act 2020; or
  • (b) a rule relating to a court or tribunal or the procedure, practice or costs of a court or tribunal; or
  • (c) an instrument or a class of instruments prescribed to be a statutory rule or statutory rules under section 4(1)(a); or
  • (d) an instrument or class of instrument that is deemed to be a statutory rule or statutory rules by the authorising Act— but does not include an instrument or class of instrument specified in paragraph (a) or (b) which is exempted under section 4(1)(b) or referred to in subsection (1A).

7. Currently, no regulations have been made under section 4(1)(a) or (b) of the SLA.

8. Subsection (1A) relates to rules made under the Statewide Treaty Act 2025 and provides:

(1A) An internal rule within the meaning of the Statewide Treaty Act 2025 or a substantive rule within the meaning of that Act is not a statutory rule.

1.5: Matters suitable for statutory rules

19. If possible, matters of detail subject to frequent change should be dealt with by subordinate legislation rather than primary legislation. Matters of policy and general principle should be dealt with in primary legislation, that is, in an Act. Significant matters should not be included in subordinate legislation, although subordinate legislation may deal with the same issue in terms of enforcement or related matters.

20. Subordinate legislation must be consistent with the general objectives of the authorising Act. Statutory rules can complete the details of a legislative scheme but cannot add new aims or ideas unless expressly authorised so to do.

21. The Subordinate Legislation Guidelines list the following matters that should be in primary legislation rather than subordinate legislation—

● matters of substance or important procedural matters (particularly if they also affect individual rights and liberties, e.g. provisions that reverse the onus of proof or certify evidentiary matters);

● matters relating to a significant question of policy in that they introduce new policy or fundamentally change existing policy;

● matters which have a significant impact on individual rights and liberties (e.g. powers of entry and search, arrest warrants, seizure and forfeiture), or which deal with property rights or traditional liberties and freedoms;

● matters imposing significant criminal penalties (such as fines exceeding 20 penalty units or imprisonment);

● provisions imposing taxes.

22. By contrast, the following are more appropriately dealt with by subordinate legislation—

● matters relating to detailed implementation of policy, general principles and standards (rather than the policy, principle or standard itself);

● prescribing fees to be paid for various services;

● prescribing forms (if it is necessary that they be prescribed) for use in connection with legislation;

● prescribing processes relating to the enforcement of legal rights and obligations under the primary legislation.

23. The decision as to whether statutory rules or another form of subordinate instrument, such as a legislative instrument, is used is generally determined at the time a Bill for an Act is being drafted.

1.6: Manner of making statutory rules

24. The authorising Act will provide for the manner of making statutory rules. Current and standard practice is that statutory rules which are regulations are made the Governor in Council. This reflects the SLA.

25. Any other mechanism needs to be carefully considered at the time an Act is prepared as a Bill and should be discussed with OCPC.

26. The introductory words at the beginning of the statutory rule should indicate the method by which it is to be made and reflect the words of the regulation-making section of the Act under which they are made. You should ensure that this is checked before sending draft rules to OCPC for settling and the allocated drafter will review this in settling.

27. Generally, all statutory rules which are made or approved by the Governor in Council are made on the recommendation of the responsible Minister, consistent with the SLA.

Part 2: Consultation requirements under the SLA

28. Legislation officers will need to be familiar with all the requirements of the SLA for the making of statutory rules. You are referred to the Subordinate Legislation Guidelines for a more comprehensive review of these requirements. Your own Department may also have specific requirements of which you need to be aware. See also the summary of certificates required before making of a statutory rule in Part 6 below.

2.1: Regulatory Impact Statements (RIS)

29. Section 7 of the SLA requires that, unless an exemption certificate is issued for a statutory rule, a RIS (regulatory impact statement) must be prepared. Sections 10 and 11 of the SLA set out the requirements for a RIS including the requirements for advertising and calling for submissions and comments.

30. BRV publishes guidance material on the RIS process and on what constitutes an appreciable economic or social burden on a sector of the public. The Subordinate Legislation Guidelines also provide details about the requirements for and development of RISs.

31. A RIS must:

● state the objectives of the proposed statutory rule;

● explain the effect of the rule;

● if the proposed statutory rule amends fees, include a table which compares the proposed fees with the existing fees. The table should also include the percentage increase or decrease for each fee;

● identify alternative practicable means of achieving these objectives;

● assess the costs and benefits of the rule and the alternatives;

● include the reasons why the alternatives or other means are not appropriate; and

● include any other matters specified by the Subordinate Legislation Guidelines

● includes a copy of the settled proposed statutory rule.

32. Section 10(3) of the SLA requires the Minister to ensure that independent advice as to the adequacy of the RIS and of the assessment included in the RIS is obtained and considered in accordance with the Subordinate Legislation guidelines. This is normally the BRV assessment.

33. The settled proposed statutory rule is the draft statutory rule as settled by OCPC. You will be given a formal settling letter on OCPC letterhead for this purpose. This is not the same as the section 13 certificate.

34. Section 11 of the SLA requires the responsible Minister to ensure publication in the Government Gazette and in a daily newspaper (this latter requirement can be met by publication on the Victorian Public Notices website under section 38M of the Interpretation of Legislation Act 1984) and, if appropriate, in other journals or publications) of a notice of the preparation of the RIS. This notice must include:

● the reason for the proposed statutory rule and the objectives of it,

● a summary of the RIS's results;

● advice about where copies of the RIS and the proposed statutory rule can be obtained; and

● an invitation for comments and submissions on the statement within 28 days or a longer period specified in the notice (60 days is considered best practice: see the Subordinate Legislation Guidelines)

35. The publication of this notice has to be at least 8 weeks before the proposed statutory rule is to be made as at least 28 days have to be allowed for public submissions (section 11(2)(d) SLA). Depending on internal time constraints, Departments should also allow at least one week for consideration of submissions by the Department and at least 2 weeks must be allowed for finalising proofs and submission copies and for the Chief Parliamentary Counsel to give a section 13 certificate. Urgent requests will be prioritised.

36. Any changes made to the statutory rule as a result of the RIS process will need to be settled by OCPC if the changes involve new material.

37. Section 12 of the SLA requires notice of the decision to make the proposed statutory rule to be published in the Government Gazette and in a daily newspaper (see also section 38M ILA) before the statutory rule is submitted to the Governor in Council for making.

38. A copy of the RIS and the compliance certificate under section 10(4) of the SLA from the responsible Minister specifying that the relevant requirements for RISs have been complied with and that, in the Minister's opinion, the statement adequately assesses the likely impact of the statutory rule must be sent to the SARC as soon as practicable after the statutory rule is made, together with the other documentation required under the SLA. (See section 15A SLA).

2.2: Exemption from RIS

39. The SLA sets out the grounds on which statutory rules may be exempted from the requirements of a RIS and provides that exempt regulations must have exemption certificates from the responsible Minister and, in certain cases, from the Premier.

2.3: Minister's exemption certificate (section 8 SLA)

40. The responsible Minister may issue an exemption certificate specifying that, in the Minister's opinion—

● the proposed statutory rule would not impose a significant economic or social burden on a sector of the public [section 8(1)(a)]

● the proposed statutory rule is a rule which relates only to a court or tribunal or the procedure, practice or costs of a court or tribunal [section 8(1)(b)]

● the proposed statutory rule is of a fundamentally declaratory or machinery nature [section 8(1)(c)]

● the proposed statutory rule increases fees in respect of a financial year by an amount that does not exceed the annual rate fixed by the Treasurer in accordance with section 5 of the Monetary Units Act 2004; [section 8(1)(d)]

● the proposed statutory rule would only impose a burden on a public sector body; [section 8(1)(da)]

● the proposed statutory rule—

○ only prescribes under section 4(1)(a) of the SLA an instrument or class of instrument to be a statutory rule; [section 8(1)(e)(i)]

○ only exempts under section 4(1)(b) of the SLA an instrument or class of instrument from the operation of the Act [section 8(1)(e)(ii)]

○ is an extension regulation [section 8(1)(e)(iii)] ○ only prescribes under section 4A(1)(a) of the SLA an instrument or class of instrument for the purposes of paragraph (h) of the definition of legislative instrument; [section 8(1)(e)(iv)]

○ only prescribes under section 4A(1)(b) of the SLA an instrument or class of instrument to be, or not to be, a legislative instrument for the purposes of the SLA or any provision of the SLA [section 8(1)(e)(v)]

○ only exempts under section 4A(1)(c) an instrument or a class of instrument from the operation of the SLA or any specified provision of the SLA [section 8(1)(e)(vi)];

● the proposed statutory rule is required under a national uniform legislation scheme and an assessment of costs and benefits has been undertaken under that scheme [section 8(1)(f)]

● the proposed statutory rule:

○ does not remake (whether with or without amendment) a statutory rule that has already been made under a Premier's exemption certificate; and

○ is of not more than 12 months duration; and

○ is necessary to respond to—

○ a public emergency; or

○ an urgent public health issue or an urgent public safety issue; or

○ likely or actual significant damage to the environment, resource sustainability or the economy; [section 8(1)(fa)]

● the proposed statutory rule deals with administration or procedures within or as between—

○ Departments or declared authorities within the meaning of the Public Administration Act 2004 [section 8(1)(g)(i)]; or

○ Departments within the meaning of the Parliamentary Administration Act 2005 [section 8(1)(g)(ii)];

● notice of the proposed statutory rule would render the proposed statutory rule ineffective or would unfairly advantage or disadvantage any person likely to be affected by the proposed statutory rule [section 8(1)(h)].

2.4: Premier's exemption certificate (section 9 SLA)

41. The Premier may issue an exemption certificate under section 9(1) of the SLA if, in the Premier's opinion, in the special circumstances of the case, the public interest requires that the proposed statutory rule be made without a RIS. The Premier must not issue a certificate unless—

● the proposed statutory rule is to expire on or before the day which is 12 months after the first day on which any provision of the statutory rule is to come into operation; and

● the responsible Minister has given the Premier written reasons why the public interest requires that the proposed statutory rule be made without a RIS [section 9(2) SLA].

42. A Premier's exemption certificate must specify the reasons for the exemption and must be signed by the Premier and be dated with the date on which the certificate was signed [section 9(3) SLA].

43. See the Subordinate Legislation Guidelines for information on what is required to obtain a Premier's exemption certificate and the circumstances in which they may be given.

2.5: Competition Policy

44. Statutory rules for which a RIS is required must have a competition policy certificate. See Subordinate Legislation Guidelines, paragraph 241. That certificate must state that the proposed statutory rule:

● does not contain a restriction on competition; or

● does contain a restriction or restrictions on competition but the objectives of the policy, decision or action cannot be achieved by another approach that:

○ does not restrict competition or lessens the restriction on competition; and

○ has net community benefits that are at least as high.

45. The competition policy certificate, with the documentation of the assessment attached, must be provided to the Clerk of the Executive Council. See the Subordinate Legislation Guidelines.

46. Proposed statutory rules which have a Premier's exemption certificate under section 9 of the SLA from the RIS process are not required to comply with the competition policy assessment requirements.

47. For further information contact BRV.

Part 3: OCPC's requirements

3.1: Draft statutory rule for settling

48. Generally, drafts of proposed statutory rules which are regulations are prepared by Departmental legislation officers. In limited circumstances, drafting of statutory rules may be undertaken by OCPC and requests for drafting of statutory rules by OCPC should be made to the Chief Parliamentary Counsel.

49. Rules of Court, VCAT Rules and other tribunal rules are drafted by OCPC on the instruction of the appropriate Court or Tribunal or the relevant Rules Committee.

50. Amending regulations will generally amend a single set of principal regulations and do not follow the "omnibus" amending style that is often seen in Bills. This practice is intended to make the amended law easier for users to find and reflects the scheme of the SLA. It prevents complicating the objectives provisions of amending regulations and simplifies the issuing of a section 13 certificate and other certificates under the SLA.

51. Omnibus amending regulations may be permitted if they are being made under the same single authorising Act and the amendments are related or consequential in nature e.g. a new scheme has been established impacting on more than one set of Regulations made under the same authorising Act. In this case, a separate part is used for each set of Principal Regulations being amended by the omnibus regulations. The title must include the title of each Principal Regulations being amended.

52. A copy of a proposed statutory rule in draft form prepared in Word should be sent to the Chief Parliamentary Counsel by email for settling advice:

● at least 6 to 8 weeks before it is proposed to be made; or

● if a RIS is required, at least 6 to 8 weeks before the statement is proposed to be published.

53. If a shorter timeline is needed, please advise the Chief Parliamentary Counsel. If they are large or complex, a longer settling period may be required.

54. A covering letter or email should be included which sets out a brief explanation of the background about the need for the statutory rule and its intended effect. The following information assists OCPC to settle the draft statutory rule—

● a brief outline of the proposed statutory rule;

● a brief explanation of the changes effected by the proposed statutory rule and the reasons for making it;

● the contact details of the officer responsible for the draft statutory rule;

● whether a RIS will be prepared (this assists us in timing and resource allocation);

● a general outline of the timelines for making the rule. If the statutory rule is urgent, please advise us of this so it can be appropriately prioritised.

55. The proposed statutory rule is then registered at OCPC and allocated by the Chief Parliamentary Counsel to one or more drafters for review and settling. You will be sent a letter by email advising you of the person responsible for settling the statutory rule and, unless the statutory rule is urgent, indicating a date by which you may expect a first response.

3.2: Settling process

56. OCPC will consider the draft as to whether it is within power under the Act which authorises its making and is suitable as to form and content. In undertaking this process, OCPC considers (among other things) all the criteria set out in section 13 of the SLA, being whether the proposed statutory rule—

● appears to be within the powers conferred by the authorising Act;

● appears without clear and express authority being conferred by the authorising Act—

○ to have a retrospective effect; or

○ to impose a tax, fee, fine, imprisonment or other penalty; or

○ to shift the legal burden of proof to a person accused of an offence; or

○ to sub-delegate powers delegated by the authorising Act;

● appears to be consistent with the general objectives of the authorising Act;

● appears to be consistent with and to achieve the objectives set out in the proposed statutory rule and, if the proposed statutory rule is to amend an existing statutory rule, appears to be consistent with the objectives set out in the existing statutory rule;

● appears to be inconsistent with principles of justice and fairness;

● appears significantly or substantially to overlap or conflict with any other statutory rule or legislation;

● is expressed as clearly and unambiguously as is reasonably possible.

57. OCPC will also consider whether the statutory rule can be interpreted consistently with the Charter Act, in accordance with section 32 of the Charter Act.

58. You will receive a letter of advice by email which may include your draft with notations or tracked changes as to any changes required. These changes may involve substantive and other technical changes required to make the draft legally effective and compliant with section 13 SLA. You may also receive suggested draft provisions for inclusion in your next draft. You should incorporate the changes required and send a revised draft back by email for further settling, as required by the drafter dealing with your file. If the statutory rule is particularly complex or issues as to power require discussion, a meeting may be arranged.

59. Once the content is satisfactory to us and to you, and within the powers in the authorising Act, the proposed statutory rule is "settled" and you will receive a settling letter to this effect.

60. If the proposed statutory rule is subject to RIS, you can use the settled draft in your format for the RIS or, if necessary (if the document is already on OCPC's system or if you wish the document be in a form closer to the printed style of legislation), you can request an exposure draft to be prepared. Note that only a pdf of the exposure draft will be provided to you. You may request a Word version for use on your website for accessibility purposes, but such a Word version must not be altered by you. It is imperative for the integrity of the document and legislation generally that authorial control is maintained by OCPC at all times.

61. If a RIS is required, the proposed statutory rule to be published with the RIS must be settled by OCPC before the RIS is published. The compliance certificate under section 10(4) of the SLA should not be issued unless the proposed rule has been settled by OCPC. BRV will also need to know that the statutory rule has been settled before signing off on the RIS for advertising and will expect to see the settling letter from OCPC for this purpose.

62. If the proposed rule is altered during any stage of the RIS process, it must be submitted to OCPC for resettling before it is prepared by OCPC for making.

63. If you are not undertaking a RIS, we then require a Word format electronic copy to be emailed to us of the final settled version so that the document can be put into OCPC's templates for preparation for making.

3.3: Printing

64. Before a statutory rule is made, it must be prepared for printing. OCPC arranges the preparation of a proof copy and when the proof copy is satisfactory, the preparation of the submission copy which is submitted to the Governor in Council (or other rule making body) for the making of the statutory rule.

3.4: Request for proof copy

65. Once the draft statutory rules has been settled by the relevant drafter and finally approved by the Department or Minister, the drafter will arrange for a proof of the statutory rules which is when the Departmental draft is moved to the customised Word template by OCPC. If a clean copy of the draft has not already been provided, the drafter will request the legislation officer to email a clean version of the draft in electronic form (Word).

66. The drafter returns the proof copy in pdf to the legislation officer for checking. This may accompany the settling letter or be provided separately by email. The legislation officer must proof-read the proof copy, ensure that the proof copy satisfies all the Department's policy requirements and return the checked proof copy, with any corrections hand-marked on it or advised as drafting instructions, to the drafter dealing with the file in OCPC. If there are any significant changes, a further proof will be provided. The proof copy is also checked by OCPC's legislative services staff at this stage as an additional quality assurance check. The drafter will advise the legislation officer if anything of substance is identified by this check.

67. Departments should not make any electronic changes to proof copies provided to them in an electronic format (pdf). Proof copies are confidential proofs provided by OCPC. Departments should not attempt or purport to provide altered proofs even if they are only for use within the Department. The proofs are set up in OCPC's format and altering the draft electronically interferes with the integrity of the document and the maintenance by OCPC of authorial control, including publication of the legislation to the legislation website.

3.5: Request for submission copy

68. When the final proof copy is settled, the legislation officer should advise the drafter that the proof copy is satisfactory and satisfies the requirements of the Department. If the legislation officer is satisfied that the statutory rule has been, or will be, approved by the Minister and the Secretary of the Department, the officer should request Parliamentary Counsel to arrange for the preparation of a submission copy of the statutory rule and a section 13 certificate. That is, you can request the section 13 certificate at the same time as you request the preparation of the submission copy. It is called a submission copy as this is the copy of the statutory rule submitted to the Governor in Council (or other rule maker) for the making of the rule.

3.6: Section 13 certificate

69. Ideally, a request for a section 13 certificate should be made at least 2 weeks before the date on which it is proposed to submit the statutory rule to the Minister for recommendation for making but urgent requests can be accommodated. You need to allow additional time to obtain any necessary Departmental approvals and the Minister's recommendation. Please let us know if it is urgent and we will prioritise it. The request should be made on the basis of a satisfactory proof copy.

70. If the statutory rule is to be first made or approved by a Board, Committee or other body, the request should be made allowing sufficient time for the scheduled meetings of the Board, Committee or other body and any necessary consideration within the Department or by the Minister before submission of the rule to the Board, Committee or other body.

71. A request to the Chief Parliamentary Counsel for a section 13 certificate to be issued must be in writing on letterhead (which can be emailed to OCPC), as the provision of a section 13 certificate is the exercise of a function under a statutory requirement. An e-mail request without the letter may be accepted at the discretion of the drafter or Chief Parliamentary Counsel, for example in very urgent cases, but must be from a person with sufficient authority to authorise the preparation of submission copies. A request must be accompanied by, or refer to, a clean, unaltered final proof of the proposed statutory rule or a proof subject to specified minor alterations, if necessary.

72. OCPC will arrange for the preparation of a submission copy of the statutory rule and of a section 13 certificate. This is then submitted to the Chief Parliamentary Counsel for signing.

73. The section 13 certificate is for the statutory rule to which it relates and the circumstances as at the date of the certificate. It has no validity except in relation to that submission copy.

74. Once the section 13 certificate has been signed, the drafter will email to the Clerk of the Executive Council the signed section 13 certificate and the related submission copy of the statutory rule in accordance with of the Executive Council's electronic lodgement procedures.

75. The drafter will send to you as the legislation officer the signed Chief Parliamentary Counsel's letter regarding the signing the section 13 certificate and its lodgement by OCPC with Executive Council and an electronic copy of the submission copy for your records. Once you receive this notification, you should lodge all your other documentation required for the making of the statutory rule with the Clerk of the Executive Council if you have not already done so. See section 14 SLA. That is, the section 13 certificate and the submission copy of the statutory rule meet up with your other SLA documentation at Executive Council.

76. The submission copy sent to the Clerk of the Executive Council is, in effect, the master copy used by the Governor in Council for making the statutory rules and OCPC for preparing the publication version of the made statutory rule.

77. If a proposed statutory rule needs to be altered after the section 13 certificate is issued or amendments are needed, a new submission copy and a new section 13 certificate are required before the statutory rule is re-submitted to the Clerk of the Executive Council for making by the Governor in Council. This requirement does not apply if the alteration is only in the introductory words (e.g. in the name or portfolio of the responsible Minister or if the Lieutenant-Governor or Administrator is acting for the Governor). Such a change will normally be made by the Clerk of the Executive Council by hand.

78. If amendments are needed, the Department should email OCPC with a request for a new submission copy, specifying the alterations to the statutory rule and requesting a revised section 13 certificate. If the amendments are substantial, OCPC may prepare a fresh proof for you to check first.

79. It is the Department's responsibility to ensure that the statutory rule is made as soon as practicable after the issue of the section 13 certificate. If there is to be any significant delay in making the statutory rule or the Department becomes aware of any relevant change in circumstances after the issue of the certificate, the matter should be discussed with OCPC.

80. If the section 13 certificate is issued towards the end of a year and the statutory rule is not made before the end of the year, you will need to send it back to OCPC for the statutory rule to be reprinted with the new year and the section 13 certificate is then reissued.

81. A section 13 certificate may contain one or more of the following notes or qualifications—

● for statutory rules with a commencement provision, a time limit as to the advice about retrospectivity.

● for statutory rules made under section 5A of the Subordinate Legislation Act 1994, a time limit as to the advice about power to make an extending statutory rule i.e. it must be made before the sunset date.

● for statutory rules containing incorporated material, a note that the certificate does not apply to any of the incorporated material.

● where amendments also amend the existing objectives of a principal statutory rule.

82. Other qualifications may be included if circumstances so require.

Part 4: Form and content of statutory rules

4.1: Language and style

83. A statutory rule must be expressed—

● in language that is clear and unambiguous;

● in a way which ensures that its meaning is certain and there are no inconsistencies;

● in language that gives effect to its stated purpose;

● consistently with the language of the authorising Act; and

● in accordance with plain English drafting standards in accordance with OCPC practice.

84. A statutory rule should—

● not duplicate, overlap or conflict with other statutory rules or legislation;

● clearly set out as part of its text—

○ the objectives of the rule; and

○ the precise provision authorising the rule;

● not deal with matters outside the scope of its objectives.

85. The format and publication style of a statutory rule is standardised and OCPC will prepare all proofs (and exposure drafts if necessary) for Departments in accordance with our customised template. In preparing your first draft, the format of current statutory rules should be followed as closely as possible by Departments so that your first draft rules are in accordance with the structure, format and standard provisions of current statutory rules.

86. For further assistance see—

● Appendix 1 which contains notes about common provisions in statutory rules and the order of those provisions.

● Appendix 2 which sets out some examples of the style and form of those provisions.

● Appendix 3 which sets out examples of the OCPC amending style. The style of amending statutory rules should generally follow that used for Acts with some exceptions.

4.2: Some general tips

87. This is not an exhaustive list and but when drafting proposed statutory rules, you should keep the following in mind—

● What regulations are needed to complete the scheme of the Act?

● Use the active voice, present tense, singular

● Use "must" not "shall"

● Use "if" not "where" if you are referring to a condition

● Structure your draft in a logical order, for example follow the provisions or structure of the authorising Act if appropriate or divide your draft into topic-specific Parts if it is a long document

● Use useful headings. If you are inserting a new regulation, use the heading "New regulation 24A inserted". If you are substituting, use "Regulation 24A substituted". If you are making a small amendment to an existing regulation, use the heading of the existing regulation which you are amending so that the reader knows the subject matter of the amendment and the provision into which it is going

● Break up the text: consider the length of provisions, paragraphs etc

● Offences

○ Make elements of the offence clear

○ What is the activity to be controlled or prohibited? Who is to be controlled?

○ Use separate regulations or separate subregulations for each separate offence, especially if you intend to use infringement notices to enforce them

● Use notes and examples if appropriate, but sparingly. You should focus on making the provision itself clear.

4.3: Authorising Act and Charter Act

88. In preparing your draft statutory rule, you need to be familiar with the content and scheme of the Act which authorises the making of the statutory rule. You need to consider what matters need to be included in the statutory rule to complete the scheme or allow for the effective implementation of it, and whether there is sufficient power in the Act to support all the things you wish to include in the proposed statutory rule.

89. You will also need to consider the impact of the Charter Act on your proposed statutory rule and whether those rules could engage rights under the Charter Act. Section 32 of the Charter Act requires all legislation to be interpreted consistently with the Charter Act, so far as is consistent with the legislation's purpose. This may impact on the authorising powers under which you are making the statutory rule or the statutory rule itself. A statutory rule may be invalid if it is inconsistent with the Charter Act and not authorised by its empowering Act so to be. (Section 32(3)(b) of the Charter Act).

90. A human rights assessment has to be undertaken in preparing draft statutory rules as to Charter rights and you should refer to the Guidelines on the Charter Act prepared by the Department of Justice and Community Safety. Under section 12A of the SLA, every statutory rule has to have a human rights certificate, or, in very limited cases, a human rights exemption certificate before it can be made.

4.4: Commencement

91. Best practice is that a specific commencement date is set out in the statutory rule to provide advance notice of the statutory rule, certainty of its commencement and to ensure the statutory rule can take full effect after allowing for all publication and notification requirements under the SLA.

92. A statutory rule or a provision of a statutory rule comes into operation at the beginning of the day on which the statutory rule is made or at the beginning of such later day as is expressed in the statutory rule as the day on which the statutory rule or provision comes into operation. (SLA, section 16).

93. Section 13 of the ILA provides that subordinate instruments can be made and brought into operation in limited circumstances between Royal Assent of the relevant authorising Act or provisions and the commencement of that authorising Act or provision. It is generally recommended that Departments do not rely on section 13 for this purpose except in exceptional circumstances. That section also empowers various administrative and machinery matters to be undertaken prior to the commencement of provisions of an Act.

94. Generally, OCPC will try to ensure that the making and commencement of the statutory rule coincide with the proclamation and commencement of the authorising Act or provision by advising the Clerk of the Executive Council about proclamation of commencement of Acts. If a Department has a commencement issue, this should be raised with OCPC in the course of settling the statutory rule.

95. Draft proclamations of commencement for Acts should be sent to Catherine Schipano, Deputy Chief Parliamentary Counsel or Catriona Duncan, Senior Parliamentary Counsel for settling.

96. A statutory rule cannot be retrospective in effect unless this is expressly authorised by the authorising Act.

97. Sections 16 and 20 of the SLA provide for the limited effect of statutory rules despite their coming into operation. A person cannot be convicted of an offence or prejudicially affected or subjected to a liability if it is proved that the statutory rule has not been printed and published by the Government Printer or if a Gazette notice of availability has not been published in the Government Gazette. However, these limitations will not apply if reasonable steps have been taken to bring the purport of the statutory rule to the notice of the public or persons affected (see s 16(3) SLA). Section 20 provides for the availability of a statutory rule for purchase or inspection.

4.5: Forms, plans, maps etc.

98. If a form, plan, map or other art work is required to be included in a statutory rule (whether in a schedule or otherwise)—

● any form must clearly and succinctly set out the information to be given by, or in response to, the form;

● any form issued to the public under the authority of a statutory rule must be in or to the like effect of the prescribed form but does not need to be identical with it (section 53, ILA). Issued forms may be larger, may include details of the address, telephone number or websites of a Department, instructions for filling in the forms or other additional but subsidiary information and may be designed with boxes, lines, colours or other aids.

99. The forms set out in the statutory rule should not include this additional material, if it is not necessary to understand what the form means or to meet the requirements of the authorising Act.

● reduced photocopies of forms, plans, maps etc., prepared for other purposes must not be used for inclusion in statutory rules unless the reduction in no way detracts from the clarity of the original;

● a plan or map should be an original prepared by or under the authority of the Surveyor-General suitable for publication in the rule;

● a plan or map should include a scale and should readily identify the location of the area.

100. Any doubts about the design or suitability of a form, plan, map or other art work should be discussed with OCPC. If possible, images should be sent to OCPC in .jpg format to avoid problems in publishing the images.

4.6: What is Incorporated Material?

101. Under section 32 of the ILA, a subordinate instrument (a statutory rule or other instrument that is made under an Act and contains regulations, rules, by-laws, proclamations, Orders in Council, orders or schemes or is otherwise of a legislative character) may apply, adopt or incorporate provisions of:

● a Victorian Act,

● a Commonwealth Act,

● a Victorian statutory rule

● a Commonwealth statutory rule

● various Codes and Laws specified in section 32(1) of the ILA.

102. If the authorising Act under which a statutory rule is made specifically authorises it, the statutory rule may apply, adopt, or incorporate provisions of any other document, for example an Australian Standard.

103. If you propose that a statutory rule will apply, adopt or incorporate material, the Department must consider the following—

● there must be express power in the authorising Act to empower the application, adoption or incorporation of material in a statutory rule;

● members of the public affected by the rule must be able to access the incorporated document so that they can understand the contents and effect of the rule;

● whether the incorporated material is readily available at a reasonable cost;

● the requirements set out in section 32 of the Interpretation of Legislation Act 1984 which are designed to facilitate Parliamentary oversight of incorporation of material and to ensure that the material is publicly available.

104. Remember that the incorporated material may not be a self-contained document and may apply, adopt or incorporate other material.

105. In drafting a statutory rule which refers to an Australian Standard, consideration should be given as to whether the reference to an Australian Standard should be to a specific standard or to a specific version of a standard by reference to its date. This may be constrained by the empowering provisions in the authorising Act. Some Acts authorise incorporation of a document as in force from time to time, whereas others are more limited and only permit incorporation of a document as at the date of publication of the document or the date of making of the statutory rule.

106. In deciding whether to incorporate material by reference, Departments need to take care to balance the drafting convenience with ease of access to the incorporated material and understanding of it by those affected by it or required to comply with it. Departments should reserve the use of incorporated detailed and extensive technical material to statutory rules concerning industries familiar with and using the material. In such cases Departments should also consider whether performance standards are a more appropriate means of regulation.

107. If you incorporate material such as Australian Standards, you will need to provide a copy of that material to OCPC during the course of settling the proposed statutory rule as OCPC need to be satisfied that there is power to incorporate material and will need to check the citation and publication details to ensure compliance with the requirements of the Subordinate Legislation Regulations.

108. OCPC acknowledge access to such material may be difficult to obtain due to licensing issues. Discuss this with the drafter if this arises as we may be able to access it by other means, however, you will need the material for tabling in Parliament and for compliance with section 32 ILA so if it cannot be accessed for production to OCPC, you may need to reconsider incorporating it.

109. OCPC will also check that the document is suitable for incorporation in a law but, due to the technical nature of some of these documents, we can only examine the documents from the perspective of whether or not it sets out a clear obligation if offences flow from contravention of the incorporated material, rather than recommendations which are not enforceable or certain.

110. A section 13 certificate cannot be issued if a document incorporated in a statutory rule is not in existence at the date of settling of the statutory rule or at the latest, the request for the section 13 certificate.

Part 5: Sunsetting and extension Regulations

5.1: Sunsetting statutory rules: 10 year expiry

131. Section 5 of the SLA provides for the automatic revocation of a statutory rule 10 years after it is made. Section 5, when read with sections 25 and 44(2) of the ILA, arguably means that statutory rules are revoked at the end of the relevant day but there is some ambiguity about this. Best practice is to ensure the replacement statutory rule or the extension regulations are in place before the automatic revocation occurs and the day before the sunset date should be the date of commencement of remade statutory rules at the latest. This ensures a seamless transition.

132. Departments should maintain accurate records of the sunset dates for all statutory rules administered by the Minister to whom the Department reports. It is essential that a Department allow sufficient time for the review of the continuing appropriateness of the statutory rules if they are to be made in whole, in part or in a modified form. Departments should keep records of these revocation dates for all statutory rules administered by the Department. Procedures for reviewing and remaking these statutory rules need to be in place to ensure that any necessary statutory rules are in force.

133. Departments should notify OCPC of the Minister's intentions about remaking any statutory rule that is due to sunset and should notify OCPC at least 6 months before the sunset date so that OCPC is in a position to provide timely advice in settling the proposed new rule.

134. BRV have a list of sunsetting statutory rules on their website, usually for the next 2 years. OCPC also has a list of these on our website and we have a program of notifying Departments to remind them of sunsetting statutory rules and seeking an update of intentions to remake or allow statutory rules to expire.

135. When drafting new statutory rules which are to replace rules due to sunset, you need to review the entire document to take into account any changes that have occurred in the past 10 years both to your authorising Act and to other Acts referred to in the document. Redundant matters should be omitted and the document renumbered with consecutive numbers.

136. The old Principal statutory rule will need to be revoked, as will all amending statutory rules which have altered the Principal statutory rule over the past 10 years and which are still in force.

5.2: Extending the operation of sunsetting rules (extension regulations)

137. A statutory rule may be extended in operation for a period not exceeding 12 months if the responsible Minister certifies that, due to special circumstances, there is insufficient time to comply with Part 2 of the SLA before the rule is due to be revoked. Before an extension certificate can be issued, the responsible Minister must obtain a certificate of agreement from the Premier [section 5A(3) of the SLA].

138. Only one extension of a statutory rule can be made [section 5A(5)].

139. When drafting an extension regulation, best practice is to fix the date for the beginning of the extension period as the day before the sunset date (and in any event, not later than the sunset date) (see section 44 of the ILA) and in all cases, fix the end of extension period as at least one day before the 12 month anniversary of the sunset date of the statutory rule to be extended. This is to ensure the 12 month extension period is not exceeded.

140. In order to extend a statutory rule, the responsible Minister must issue certificates under sections 5A and 8 of the SLA. A certificate of agreement from the Premier is also required under section 5A(3) of the SLA and legislation officers should contact DPC Office of General Counsel in sufficient time for this to be agreed to and obtained.

141. The title of extension regulations should follow the naming convention Subordinate Legislation (ABC Regulations 2016) Extension Regulations 2026 (see definition of "extension regulation" in section 3 of the SLA).

● that is a rule which relates only to a court or tribunal or the procedure, practice or costs of a court or tribunal; (court rules); or

● that prescribes under section 4(1)(a) of the Act an instrument or class of instrument to be a statutory rule (bringing an instrument under the operation of the SLA); or

● that exempts under section 4(1)(b) of the Act an instrument or class of instrument from the operation of the SLA; or

○ that prescribes under section 4A(1)(a) of the SLA an instrument or class of instrument for the purposes of paragraph (h) of the definition of legislative instrument; or ○ that prescribes under section 4A(1)(b) of the SLA an instrument or class of instrument to be, or not to be, a legislative instrument for the purposes of the SLA or any provision of the SLA; or

● that is an extension regulation.

6.7: Composite certificates

162. Section 12B of the SLA provides that all or any of the following certificates may be included in a composite certificate—

● a certificate of consultation [section 6 of the SLA]

● an infringement certificate [section 6A of the SLA]

● an exemption certificate issued by the responsible Minister [section 8 of the SLA]

● a certificate that the requirements relating to a RIS have been met [section 10(4) of the SLA].

6.8: Premier's exemption certificate

163. Section 9 of the SLA gives the Premier the power to exempt a proposed statutory rule from the RIS process where the Premier is of the opinion that, in the special circumstances of the particular case, the public interest requires that the proposed statutory rule be made without complying with the RIS process under section 7 of the SLA. This power is for use in cases of emergency or overriding public interest. Section 9(2)(b) of the SLA requires that the relevant responsible Minister has given the Premier written reasons why the public interest requires that the proposed statutory rule be made without complying with section 7(1).

164. Departments should not seek the Premier's views as to the granting of an exemption certificate unless there are overwhelming grounds on which the Premier could reach the opinion that in the special circumstances of the particular case the public interest requires that the proposed rule be made without complying with the RIS process.

165. Subject to DPC's requirements, a copy of the settled statutory rule should be provided with the request for the Premier's exemption certificate together with a copy of the section 13 certificate provided by the Chief Parliamentary Counsel unless the request is in parallel with settling the rule due to time constraints. The application for a Premier's exemption certificate must set out evidence of the public interest to enable the Premier to form the requisite opinion on its merits.

166. The Premier's power to grant exemptions is not intended to operate as an alternative means of making statutory rules or circumventing the RIS process. Contact should be made with DPC OGC as to DPC's specific requirements. See also the Subordinate Legislation Guidelines.

167. Departments should not expect that a 12 month period will automatically be the period allowed. Premier's exemption certificates are generally given for a period not exceeding either 6 or 12 months. A statutory rule made under a Premer's exemption certificate will normally include the word "Interim" in the title to indicate the short-term operation of the rule. For example "ABC Interim Regulations 2027".

1: Title

1. A short title for a statutory rule is not necessary, see section 19 of the Subordinate Legislation Act 1994. If the rule has no short title, the rule can be cited by the title appearing before the formal or introductory parts and that title will form part of the statutory rule. This is now the standard form.

2. Wherever possible, the title of the Regulations should include the name of the authorising Act (eg. Regulations under the Bees Act 2020 should be called Bees Regulations 2020).

3. The title should be as concise as possible (e.g. "Bees (Committees) Regulations" rather than "Bees (Committee of Management and Advisory Committee) Regulations)". The primary purpose of the title is to identify rather than describe. The title should be factual and neutral.

4. The title for an amending statutory rule should generally include a reference to "Amendment" to distinguish it from principal regulations. eg "Bees (Committees) Amendment Regulations". Generally, the style should follow the style of amending Bills if possible. If you have any queries regarding the title, contact the Chief Parliamentary Counsel or discuss it with the drafter settling the draft statutory rule.

5. The title for an amending statutory rule should not include a reference to an amendment number. The exceptions are Court and Tribunal Rules.

6. If a statutory rule is to operate for a limited period of months or years e.g. because of the requirements for a Premier's exemption certificate under section 9 SLA, "Interim" should be included in the title immediately before "Regulations".

2: Types of clauses

Objectives

7. This clause must be included in all statutory rules (see Subordinate Legislation Guidelines).

8. This clause should be included in amending statutory rules. The subject-matter of amending rules may make it necessary to amend the objectives of the Principal Regulations. The section 13 certificate (under section 13(d) of the SLA) refers to the objectives of the principal statutory rules as amended by the amending statutory rule).

9. The statement of objectives should be concise and factual.

Authorising provision

10. This clause states the provision authorising the statutory rule and must be included.

11. The clause generally cites the general regulation-making power in the authorising Act. It may need to cite other regulation-making powers that support the making of the statutory rule, but usually the general power is sufficient. The section that actually confers the regulation making power need to be cited e.g. "The Governor in Council may make regulations..." or "the regulations may...". Other references to "prescribed matters" should not be cited. These provisions should be referred to in the substantive provision of the statutory rule that is providing for that matter e.g. "For the purposes of section X of the Act, the prescribed matter is...".

Commencement clause

12. A commencement clause is only strictly needed if the statutory rule, or any provision of it, is to commence after the date on which it is made, however it is best practice to include a commencement clause as it is helpful to the user of the legislation and gives time for the public to become aware of the new law. This can be discussed with the allocated drafter.

13. A commencement clause must specify a day on which the statutory rule or provision is to come into operation. The rule or provision comes into operation at the beginning of that day or, if no commencement is specified, at the beginning of the day on which it is made. (section 16 SLA). It is best practice to specify a date rather than an event such as the commencement of an Act so that users do not have to look for other material to know when the law starts or changes.

14. If provisions of a statutory rule are to come into operation on different days, the style is for all of the rule except the later ones to come into operation on the day of making or the first day on which any other provision takes effect. For example:

  • (1) These Regulations, except regulations 5 and 6, come into operation on 1 November 2027.
  • (2) Regulations 5 and 6 come into operation on 1 December 2028.

15. In the case of remaking sunsetting statutory rules, the date of commencement should be no later than the day before the sunsetting. When remaking sunsetting statutory rules, this date should be specified in the commencement clause.

16. In the case of extension regulations, these commence on their day of making which must be before the statutory rules they extend expire.

Principal statutory rule

17. A clause for the citation of the principal statutory rule is only required in amending statutory rules that make several amendments to a principal statutory rule. It is useful as a shortening provision to save repeating the title, particularly if it is long and there are multiple references to the principal statutory rule being amended.

18. An endnote number is placed after the title of the principal statutory rule (in the Principal Regulations clause or, if there is no Principal Regulations clause, in the first substantive clause referring to the principal statutory rule that is not the objectives clause) and an endnote added giving the SR number and the number and year of each amending statutory rule (see Subordinate Legislation Guidelines and the forms in Appendix 3).

Revocation clause

19. This clause is needed in a statutory rule making a new principal statutory rule to replace an earlier statutory rule.

20. If the earlier principal statutory rule has been amended, each amending statutory rule should also be revoked.

21. It may be appropriate to use a schedule to list the revoked statutory rules if they are numerous.

22. A revocation clause is unnecessary if the earlier principal statutory rule has been directly revoked or has expired or ceased to have effect because of the operation of another legislative instrument (for example, section 5 of the SLA or an expiry provision in the earlier principal statutory rule).

23. In the case of sunsetting statutory rules, any statutory rule amending the principal statutory rule that is sunsetting will need to be revoked.

Definition clause

24. This clause sets out any definitions used in the statutory rule when it is a principal statutory rule. They are not used for amending statutory rules.

25. Definitions are generally only necessary to remove ambiguity or as a shortening device.

26. Definitions used in the authorising Act should not be repeated in the statutory rule as they apply to the statutory rule by reason of section 23 of the ILA.

27. In a principal statutory rule, it is often necessary to define "the Act" (i.e. the authorising Act).

28. Do not define terms that do not appear in the statutory rule or only appear once or twice.

29. Do not include substantive matter in definitions.

Expiry or Revocation clause

30. This clause is needed for statutory rules that are to operate only for a limited period, e.g. for 6 or 12 months.

31. It is also included in extension regulations, fixing the day after the end of the extension period as the expiry date. This is so that these extension regulations, once spent, do not remain on the statute book for 10 years until they sunset under section 5 of the SLA. "Revocation" is preferred to "Expiry" to avoid timing ambiguities under section 44 of the ILA. Revocation occurs at the beginning of the day, expiry at the end of the day, depending on how it is expressed.

Endnotes and footnotes

32. If a statutory rule refers to another statutory rule, an endnote or footnote should be included (See Subordinate Legislation Guidelines). A number should be placed after the title of the rule referred to and an endnote or footnote added giving the number and year of the rule referred to and of each amending statutory rule. (See form 1 in Appendix 3).

33. Endnotes and footnotes can refer to reprinted statutory rules.

34. If a statutory rule identified in an endnote or footnote has been reprinted the note can refer to the reprinted statutory rule. The note should refer to the SR number and year, the reprint number and date, the last statutory rule incorporated in the reprint and the number and year of each statutory rule which has amended the statutory rule after the reprint. (See form 2 in Appendix 2). Reprints are no longer prepared so this is becoming redundant.

35. Endnotes are the preferred style and should be used unless there are exceptional circumstances.

Amending style

36. The style of amending statutory rules should generally follow that used for Acts, except "revoke" is used instead of "repeal". See Appendix 3 for details of amending styles for statutory rules.

5: Fees and charges

37. Fees or charges prescribed by a statutory rule should include the $ sign against each amount, rather than be set out in columns headed by a $ sign. A narrative format is preferred to a table to ensure effective prescription of the fee.

38. If fees or charges are fixed by reference to fee units, you should be aware of the Monetary Units Act 2004. The statutory rule should set out in an endnote an explanation about the value of a fee unit and the method of calculating fees and charges expressed in fee units. (See form 5 in Appendix 2). These will be inserted by OCPC's printing team so you do not need to worry about including it.

6: Penalties

39. If penalties are fixed by reference to penalty units, the statutory rule may set out in an endnote an explanation about the value of a penalty unit and the method of calculating penalties expressed in penalty units. (See form 5 in Appendix 2). These will be inserted by OCPC's printing team so you do not need to worry about including it.
